# After Action Review: SENRI El Niño work, first two weeks

**Status:** Draft v0.1, 18 November 2026. Written by Claude from Max
Semenchuk's dictated notes and the project record. Max will add more points;
other members are invited to add their own view (see the end).

**Period covered:** 6 November 2026 (first group call) to 18 November 2026.

**Format.** An After Action Review (AAR) asks four questions: what did we
expect, what actually happened, why the two differ, and what we will change.
The format comes from the US Army and is described in Peter Senge's *The Fifth
Discipline*. The most useful step is the third one, because comparing
expectation with reality shows blind spots before we jump to solutions. We are
running it on ourselves first, as agreed in the 13 November call between Max
and Robin.

**Sources:** [strategy](../strategy/STRATEGY.md) (v0.2, focus recommendation
in section 10), [damage and impact estimate](../analysis/DAMAGE-AND-IMPACT.md),
[team](../TEAM.md), [existing tools check](../research/EXISTING-TOOLS-CHECK.md),
[AI coordination concepts](../strategy/AI-COORDINATION-CONCEPTS.md),
[Rockefeller Cities Deliver analysis](../funding/ROCKEFELLER-CITIES-DELIVER.md),
the [El Niño site](https://citizen-infra.github.io/senri/el-nino/), the
6 November group call and the 13 November Max–Robin call.

## 1. What we expected

- **There is room for a coordination product.** We expected that a small team
  that builds with AI agents ("vibe coding") and has facilitation skills could
  find a niche in the 2026–27 El Niño response and make a real difference.
  The strategy named that niche as *open coordination infrastructure*: shared
  data, maps and agent access that help people already on the ground act
  faster.
- **Timing.** We expected our coordination work to happen either *before* the
  main crisis events (anticipatory action) or to adapt quickly to local needs
  as impacts appeared.
- **Users would be there.** Implicitly, we expected that someone in affected
  communities already does the coordinating work (for example, distributing
  aid) and would welcome automation that helps them.
- **A shared definition of success.** Each of us set our own success
  indicators on 6 November (strategy, section 3): reduced losses and adoption
  by larger partners (Max), lives saved and verified impact (Artem),
  frictionless need-to-offer matching (Graham), supporting even one person
  and open, emergent collaboration (Robin).
- **Group work.** We expected a portfolio of tools on a shared foundation,
  one repository with issues as the backlog, and a pilot chosen within two to
  three weeks.

## 2. What actually happened

**What came out of it**

- **We understand the problem much better.** An open problem map with
  66 problems and 27 linked organisations and projects, including a gaps view
  of important problems that nobody on the map works on.
- **We know where and when it hits.** An impact calendar with a "Where it
  hits" world map of affected areas by month.
- **Research collected.** A library of 31 sources linked to the problems it
  informs, plus a research import process (Robin). Almost none of it has been
  read by the group yet.
- **Funding picture.** A map of 20 potential funders, a fit analysis for the
  Rockefeller Foundation "Cities Deliver" round (deadline 29 November; needs a
  legal-entity applicant and a signed letter of intent from a city of at least
  50,000 people), and NLnet as a realistic path (deadline 3 January 2027).
- **Strategy and estimates written down.** Strategy v0.2 with a focus
  recommendation (open base plus one pilot), a damage estimate (about USD
  1 trillion of world GDP in 2027; our rough order of magnitude is about
  100,000 excess deaths), a fact-check of existing tools (the IWMI ENSO
  Outlook is real and open), and six AI coordination product concepts.
- **A shared home.** Everything moved into the public `citizen-infra/senri`
  repository with a live site, a specification process and a collaboration
  guide (Robin).
- **Small wins between people.** The first sign of members building on each
  other's ideas: Artem built *[tool name and link, Max to add]* following
  Max's suggestion. Out of the 13 November call came the AAR itself, both as
  a tool for the four of us to align expectations and as a possible product
  for local responders.

**What did not happen**

- **No users and no pilot.** We have no stakeholder on the front line. If we
  build, say, an aid-distribution app, there is currently no person in a
  community who distributes aid and is asking for automation.
- **No way into official coordination.** International coordination runs
  through bodies like the UN and its clusters. Without a mandate it is very
  hard to join, and that machinery is slow to move.
- **Little time and loose coupling.** The project gets modest time from each
  member. Several people are active in the chat, but each engages moderately
  and mostly on their own. Work is published but rarely connects. We had one
  group call; the second call and the issue backlog did not happen yet.
- **The pilot decision is still open.** The strategy asked for two or three
  pilot candidates to contact by 1 December 2026; none has been contacted.

## 3. The gap between expectation and reality, and why

| We expected | Reality | Why (our current understanding) |
|---|---|---|
| A coordination product would find users | No user is asking for it | We started from the problem and the tools, not from a person who already does the work. The people who coordinate locally are not in our networks yet. |
| We could help before the main events | We are in the middle of the event, at its peak | The El Niño was declared and peaked faster than we organised. Anticipatory windows have mostly closed, except for later impacts (hunger in East Africa March to August 2027, southern Africa from April 2027). |
| We could plug into the response | Official coordination needs a mandate | The UN system assigns roles to agencies; volunteers and small teams are not part of it. The gap is at city and volunteer level, where there are no formal AARs or shared learning. |
| A team of four would coordinate | A loosely coupled group, each moving alone | Coordination itself costs time and effort. No one is paid, there is no budget (decision D-008), and we have not yet agreed a light rhythm. |
| Research would guide choices | Research is collected but unread | Collecting is fast with AI; reading and discussing takes human time we do not have. |
| Success would be shared | Four different definitions | We wrote them down but never reconciled them; the AAR is the first tool to do that. |

The main lesson so far: **the hard part is not building tools but finding the
people who would use them and the format in which the four of us can keep
working together.**

## 4. What to change

1. **Choose a light coordination format.** Coordination is expensive, so
   design for people who drop in and out: short asynchronous AARs, small tasks
   that take five minutes, one short call at a fixed rhythm, and clear owners
   for the few things that matter.
2. **Run this AAR across the group.** Each member writes a short AAR (or
   records five minutes with an agent). Compare the expectations side by side
   and agree one shared goal.
3. **Find one real person before building more.** Ask our networks for people
   who already coordinate on the ground (for example the contact in Ethiopia
   that Max will share, island communities, or local water rationing). Build
   only what that person needs.
4. **Work at city and volunteer level, not at UN level.** That is where there
   is no formal coordination and no shared learning. Partner with an
   operational organisation where a mandate is needed.
5. **Widen the scope beyond El Niño.** The peak is now, but hunger will last
   another year, water problems may follow, and the same tools fit local
   floods and droughts unrelated to El Niño. Keep the problem map and
   calendar, but frame the product as local crisis coordination.
6. **Turn the AAR into a product candidate.** A shared, possibly anonymous
   database of local AARs, fed by a voice agent or a WhatsApp/Telegram bot,
   so that lessons and warning signs move quickly between places. Test it on
   ourselves first, then with one small group of responders.
7. **Read less, use more.** Pick the three or four sources that matter for the
   chosen pilot and summarise them in one page, instead of growing the library.
8. **Keep or drop the near deadlines on purpose.** Decide this week whether to
   pursue Rockefeller (29 November) at all, given that we have no legal entity
   and no city letter yet; decide by mid-December on NLnet (3 January).
